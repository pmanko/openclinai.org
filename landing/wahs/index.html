OpenClinAI / Projects / WA Health Summary (WAHS)

Exploratory Applied Research · University of Washington

Patient-controlled health summaries and source-linked question answering.

WA Health Summary (WAHS) is a patient-controlled health summary platform developed at the University of Washington Clinical Informatics Research Group. This exploratory project studies how individuals can bring together clinical records across separate clinics, hospitals, and laboratories, curate and share a concise summary with their care team, and ask plain-language questions about their own medical history with every statement linked to its originating record.

Project status: WAHS is an active research and development prototype. All patient records shown across this site and in recorded demonstrations are synthetic or de-identified examples.

Clinical Context

Why fragmented records make clinical connections difficult to see

Individuals managing ongoing health conditions routinely receive care across multiple organizations: primary care clinics, specialty practices, urgent care centers, independent laboratories, and inpatient hospitals. Each organization maintains its own documentation. When a patient retrieves and combines those records, the resulting file often spans hundreds or thousands of entries containing repeated laboratory panels, lengthy progress notes, and medication lists that do not fully agree across sites.

Individual records can be accurate in isolation while cross-clinic risks remain difficult to spot. Identifying those connections normally requires reviewing separate timelines and clinical notes side by side, a demanding task for patients, caregivers, and clinicians working within brief appointment windows.

Illustrative Scenario · Synthetic Patient

Reviewing Margaret's medications across clinics

Margaret is an older adult in her early eighties who receives care across several clinics, including a geriatrics practice and a sleep medicine clinic. Over time, two separate providers independently prescribed sedating medications, while another clinic continued two cholesterol medications from the same drug class. Her chart also documents a recent emergency department visit following a fall.

Every piece of that history is present in her combined record, distributed across different clinic exports and dates. When Margaret asks WAHS, "What active medications am I taking, and are there any fall or sedation risks I should ask my doctor about?", the system brings those entries together, explains the overlapping prescriptions and recent fall history in clear language, suggests specific questions for her care team, and links each statement directly to the underlying clinic record and date.

User-Facing Applications

The WAHS web application and mobile companion

WA Health Summary provides two patient-facing interfaces connected to the same underlying health record and interoperability standards. Updates made in one interface are immediately available in the other.

Web Application

WAHS web app

A comprehensive browser workspace for organizing multi-provider records and preparing clinical summaries.

  • Aggregate clinical records from multiple health systems and review conditions, medications, allergies, immunizations, and lab results in one view
  • Author personal health context, including personal health history, care goals, and care preferences
  • Curate an International Patient Summary and share it with clinicians or family members via secure link or QR code
  • Ask questions across the combined record using Ask WAHS and inspect the full clinical record behind any cited claim
Mobile Companion

WAHS mobile companion

A streamlined phone interface designed for everyday reference and point-of-care appointments.

  • Review a clear summary of active conditions, medications, allergies, and recent clinical updates
  • Record or update personal health notes from a phone so new context is reflected immediately
  • Present a shareable QR health summary during clinic, urgent care, or emergency visits
  • Use Ask WAHS to prepare for appointments and tap any numbered reference to inspect its source record

End-to-End Request Flow

What happens when a question is asked

Behind the Ask WAHS interface in both applications, the WAHS AI engine prepares the patient's record and coordinates with an open-weight language model through four structured stages.

  1. Authorized record retrieval

    Retrieval runs strictly under the signed-in user's own session credentials rather than administrative database keys. The system gathers clinical records imported from external clinics, hospitals, and laboratories alongside patient-authored notes and care goals, checking modification timestamps so newly added records or personal updates are reflected immediately.

  2. Record cleaning and prioritization

    Raw multi-provider exports contain formatting clutter, invalidated entries, and exact duplicate notes that make key clinical details harder to locate. Before an AI model reads the chart, duplicates and boilerplate are removed and entries are organized chronologically. Complete records are kept verbatim whenever space allows; on very large multi-year histories, routine repeat series are condensed while core safety items (allergies, active medications, active conditions, and implanted devices) are always preserved and any omitted sections are logged explicitly.

  3. Question-relevant clinical context

    Depending on what is asked, optional preparation modules attach structured clinical background alongside the organized chart without altering the original entries. These steps can highlight timeline intervals and laboratory trends, group medications by shared active ingredient or therapeutic class so cross-clinic duplications stand out, retrieve relevant passages from lengthy hospital or specialist notes, and attach public clinical guideline references.

  4. Source-linked synthesis and explicit abstention

    An open-weight language model generates a plain-language response in which every clinical claim carries a numbered reference tied to the specific medication order, laboratory result, or clinical note behind it, including its date and originating facility. Users can open any reference to inspect the source record directly. When a record does not contain the requested information, the system states what is absent rather than speculating.

Research & Engineering Focus

Context engineering, model orchestration, infrastructure, and guardrails

Answering questions reliably over real-world clinical records requires substantially more than connecting a chat interface to a general-purpose language model. Multi-clinic exports vary widely in size and structure, models lose focus when given noisy raw files, and patient-facing health tools require strict boundaries around data access, provenance, and clinical safety. Our work on WAHS centers on four connected areas, guided by systematic evaluation.

01 · Context Engineering

Structuring and enriching the clinical record

We study how longitudinal clinical records should be cleaned, sized, and supplemented before a model reads them. In WAHS, that includes determining how much history an AI model can attend to reliably at once, preserving safety-critical lists when multi-year exports must be condensed, and testing which supporting context modules (such as laboratory trends, standardized drug-class mappings, or targeted note passages) genuinely improve comprehension rather than adding distracting detail.

02 · Model Orchestration

Coordinating preparation, retrieval, and generation

Rather than relying on unpredictable multi-step loops in which a model decides on its own whether and how to search a patient's chart, WAHS coordinates deterministic record preparation, question-aware recovery of passages from long or condensed notes, and single-pass open-weight model generation. Because each supporting step is modular, the system can run as a minimal baseline over the organized chart alone or incorporate richer clinical context where needed.

03 · Serving Infrastructure

Responsive, portable open-weight deployment

Both patient-facing applications stay lightweight by delegating record preparation and model communication to a shared, stateless service. We run open-weight language models on dedicated university GPU servers and local workstations, structure prompts so follow-up questions reuse the already-prepared chart without reprocessing delays, and use standard interfaces so public-health or health-system partners can swap models or hosting environments without rewriting applications.

04 · Guardrails & Governance

Enforcing privacy, provenance, and clinical boundaries

Safety and privacy controls are built into the request path rather than left to prompt wording alone. Every query is restricted to the signed-in person's authorized records with no server database storing chat conversations; inline references are validated against real chart entries so every statement can be verified; undocumented procedures or conditions trigger explicit abstention instead of guesses; and outputs are framed to help individuals prepare for, never replace, discussions with a clinician.

Evaluating approaches from simple baselines to richer pipelines

Because record condensation, context modules, and model selection in WAHS are modular, we evaluate configurations side by side on shared multi-clinic and hospital test cases rather than assuming that added complexity always helps. Our evaluation harness compares raw unorganized records, clean condensed records on their own, and progressive combinations of timeline, medication-class, note-retrieval, and guideline context across multiple open-weight model sizes.

Generated answers are assessed against the underlying clinical records for factual accuracy, completeness, source-citation fidelity, response latency, and clinical safety, allowing architectural defaults to follow measured performance improvements.
