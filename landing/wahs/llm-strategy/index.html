WA Health Summary · LLM Strategy

Context engineering, condensation, and model selection for patient record questions

This page explains how the WAHS AI engine prepares a patient's record for a language model, how it adds question-specific context, how model calls are orchestrated, and how models and context sizes are chosen. It covers the reasoning behind each choice. How the system is built is on the Architecture page, and comparative results are published as evaluation reports.

Exploratory applied research, University of Washington Clinical Informatics Research Group · Written for informatics, engineering, and research reviewers

The approach in brief

  1. Prepare the record in code, not in the model. Extraction, deduplication, condensation, and retrieval are deterministic and auditable. The model sees a clean, numbered chart instead of raw FHIR JSON.
  2. Keep the record verbatim whenever it fits. Condensation only starts when a chart exceeds its budget, protects the safety-critical core first, and states exactly what was left out.
  3. Separate the stable chart from question-specific context. The chart forms a fixed prompt prefix; optional enrichers append bounded context after it. This keeps follow-up turns fast and makes every module independently testable.
  4. One model call per turn. Retrieval happens before the call, so answer quality does not depend on the model deciding to use tools.
  5. Open-weight models behind a standard interface. Three model sizes cover GPU servers through laptops, and any OpenAI-compatible host can be substituted.

Problem & Constraints

What the strategy has to handle

Heterogeneous, noisy records

A combined record can hold a few hundred entries or several thousand, spread across clinics with repeated lab panels, duplicated notes, Base64-encoded documents, and medication lists that disagree. Raw FHIR JSON spends most of its tokens on structure rather than clinical content.

Patient-facing questions

Questions come from patients and caregivers, not clinicians. Answers have to use plain language, separate active from historical items, avoid treatment instructions, and say clearly when the record does not contain something.

Verifiability

Every clinical statement must trace to a specific record entry so the patient or their clinician can check it. That rules out approaches where the model paraphrases the record before answering.

Privacy and hosting

Records must stay within university or local infrastructure, which favors open-weight models on UW GPUs or personal hardware, and per-user access enforced outside the model.

Context Engineering

How the prompt is structured

Each request is built from two regions with different jobs.

Region 1 · Stable prefix

System instructions and the condensed chart

  • The chart is rendered as numbered lines [1]..[N], each with its date and source, preceded by a Completeness Manifest at [2] that lists anything omitted.
  • The prefix depends only on the patient's data, scope, model, and budget, never on the question. Identical prefixes let the model host reuse cached attention state across turns.
Region 2 · Turn suffix

Question-specific context and the conversation

  • Enricher output (trends, terminology, retrieved passages, reference snippets) and patient-authored drafts are appended here with hard token caps.
  • Putting variable content after the chart means enrichment never alters or reorders the record itself, and each module can be switched on or off without changing anything else.

Instruction rules

The default system prompt encodes four rules that the rest of the pipeline is designed to support:

Rule Why it matters
Cite with tags onlyAnswers use [N] for chart entries, [P] for patient drafts, and [K] for public reference snippets. Restricting brackets to these tags lets clients turn every citation into a link and lets evaluators check citation validity mechanically.
Check completeness before saying "not found"The model consults the Completeness Manifest. If nothing was omitted, it can state that an item is not documented; if history was condensed or scoped, it says what is and is not visible.
Copy values and dates exactlyNumbers, units, reference ranges, and ISO dates are reproduced verbatim. Trends are not inferred from a single measurement, and relative time is anchored to the chart's latest clinical date.
Supportive, non-prescriptive framingAnswers distinguish active from stopped items, never tell the patient to change medications, and suggest questions to bring to their care team.

Condensation

Verbatim first, then structured reduction

The condenser works on extracted clinical items, not on text. It always tries to show the full record first and only reduces it as far as needed to fit the chart budget.

Tier What it does Design reason
Tier 0 · VerbatimRemoves FHIR wrapper overhead, invalidated entries, and duplicate notes, then renders every item.Most single-clinic and many multi-clinic records fit once transport noise is removed. Nothing clinical is lost, so nothing needs to be disclosed.
Tier 1 · Series foldingFolds repeated lab and vital series by code and unit into earliest, extremes, and latest values.Repeated measurements dominate large records. Folding keeps the trajectory and outliers while removing redundancy, without choosing which clinical facts matter.
Tier 2 · Safety core & note packingReserves allergies, active medications, active problems, and implanted devices; fills remaining space by recency; spreads what is left across notes.Follows the HL7 International Patient Summary idea of a minimum safe dataset. Everything omitted is counted in the manifest so the model and the reader know the chart is partial.
Why not have a model summarize the record? A model-written summary would be faster to build, but it introduces a second place where facts can be dropped or altered, its errors are hard to detect, and its output cannot be cited back to individual entries. Deterministic condensation is reproducible, testable, and keeps every line traceable.

Context Enrichment

Small, deterministic modules that add what the chart lacks

Enrichers address specific, recurring failure modes rather than general capability. Each one follows the same rules: deterministic logic, a hard token cap, output only in the turn suffix, a disclosure describing what it added, and no effect on the chart prefix. That makes each module easy to turn off and compare.

Module Problem it targets Approach
temporalModels confuse old and recent results and miscount elapsed time.States the chart's anchor date, lookback cutoffs, vaccine intervals, and computed lab changes over time.
terminology_kbBrand and generic names hide duplicate therapy; drug classes and missing reference ranges are left to model memory.Looks up ingredients, drug classes, vaccine schedules, and standard ranges in CIEL, RxNorm/ATC, CDC CVX/ACIP, and LOINC.
note_ragCondensation excerpts long notes, so relevant passages can fall outside the chart.Ranks unseen note passages against the question with BM25 and appends the best ones. Runs only when notes were excerpted.
overflow_lookupTight budgets omit structured items that a specific question needs.Retrieves omitted items matching the question and adds them back with citation numbers. Runs only when items were omitted.
guideline_kbSmaller models have less built-in clinical background.Adds short public reference snippets related to the chart's codes, cited as [K]. Off by default so its effect can be studied separately.

The hosted default, core-enriched, enables the first four. Because note_rag and overflow_lookup are self-gating, they add nothing when a chart fits verbatim, so small records pay no retrieval cost.

Model Orchestration

Deterministic preparation, then a single model call

Several orchestration patterns are common for question answering over long records. WAHS uses the simplest one that meets the constraints above.

Pattern Trade-off Position in WAHS
Raw record in a long contextSimple, but wastes tokens on JSON structure and relies on the model to find relevant facts across a very long input.Used only as a comparison baseline.
Agentic tool callingFlexible, but the model may skip a lookup, issue the wrong query, or stop early. Each step adds latency and failure points.Not used. Retrieval runs before the call instead.
Multi-model pipelines (summarize, then answer; or answer, then verify)Can catch some errors, but multiplies cost and latency, and intermediate outputs are harder to cite.Not used in the patient-facing path. Verification is handled in offline evaluation.
Deterministic preparation + single callPredictable latency, one place for the model to fail, every input inspectable through context preview.Default for all clients.

The engine exposes the same OpenAI-compatible interface it consumes, so clients, evaluation tools, and alternative model hosts are interchangeable. The Research Sandbox can send the engine-prepared prompt to a model on the user's own machine, which makes side-by-side comparisons on identical context straightforward.

Model Selection

Three open-weight tiers from one model family

WAHS uses open-weight models so records never leave university or user-controlled hardware. Using three sizes from one family (Gemma 4) keeps the chat template, tokenizer, and instruction-following behavior consistent, so prompts and condensation budgets transfer across tiers.

Alias Model Typical hardware When to use
wahs-26b (default)Gemma 4 26B-A4B, mixture of experts with about 4B active parameters per tokenShared GPU server (UW CIRG)Hosted default; deepest synthesis across long, multi-source records.
wahs-12bGemma 4 12B, denseSingle workstation GPU or Apple Silicon machineLocal or partner deployments that need strong quality on modest hardware.
wahs-e4bGemma 4 E4BLaptops and edge devicesFast, low-resource use; benefits most from enrichers that supply background knowledge.

Selection criteria for adding or replacing a model: open weights with a license that allows research deployment, reliable instruction following for citation and abstention rules, a tokenizer the engine can run offline for exact budgeting, and acceptable latency on the target hardware. Model aliases decouple clients from upstream model IDs, so a replacement only changes server configuration.

Context Sizing

Focused chart budgets inside one fixed allocation

Chart budget

Smaller and focused over larger and diluted

Filling a very long context does not guarantee the model uses it well; attention spreads thin over long inputs. WAHS defaults to a 16k-token chart budget with retrieval enrichers to recover what condensation set aside, and lets users and evaluators choose from 2k to 64k.

Model-host allocation

One fixed context size per model

Resizing the model host's context on every request forces a model reload whenever the size changes. WAHS requests one fixed 64k allocation, large enough for every supported budget plus conversation and output, so switching budgets never reloads the model and the cached chart prefix stays usable. Larger allocations were avoided because they strain memory on shared GPUs.

Implementation details, including how budgets are resolved and clamped, are under Budgets & context allocation.

Approaches We Moved Away From

What we tried and why we changed course

Earlier approach Why we moved away and what replaced it
WAHS profiles inside a shared multi-EHR hubCoupling WAHS to a general hub duplicated condensation code and tied release cycles together. Replaced by the standalone wahs-engine with no EHR or SQL dependencies.
Condensation and clinical rules in the browserThe earlier sandbox reimplemented engine logic in JavaScript and drifted from the real engine. Replaced by a sandbox page that calls an isolated engine instance and contains no clinical logic.
Rule-based cross-clinic reconciliation graphHand-written rules fit synthetic examples but did not generalize and produced bracketed text the model echoed as false citations. Quarantined behind an experimental flag; standards-based terminology lookup covers the general case.
Agentic tool loops over small summariesModels skipped needed lookups and missed cross-clinic connections. Replaced by larger verbatim-first charts plus retrieval run before the model call.
Per-request context resizing and very large allocationsCaused model reloads and memory pressure on shared GPUs. Replaced by one fixed allocation and early streaming frames that keep connections alive.

Privacy & Governance

Keep access control and disclosure outside the model

Prompts are not a security boundary. WAHS enforces per-user access in the authorization proxy and in the engine's scope checks, before any text reaches a model, and keeps conversation history out of server storage. Choices about what a patient shares with others belong in the sharing layer (SMART Health Links, with HL7 data segmentation as a future direction), not in prompt-level redaction. Every response carries an HL7 AI-Provenance record that names the model, prompt, and enrichers used and marks the output as requiring human review. Enforcement details are under Identity, state & caching.

How Approaches Are Compared

Every component can be switched off and measured

The two-region prompt, declarative profiles, and per-module toggles exist partly so that each design choice can be tested against simpler alternatives on the same cases. Evaluations run through the clinical AI validation harness on synthetic multi-clinic records and de-identified hospital records, comparing raw records, condensation alone, and combinations of enrichers across model sizes and chart budgets. Answers are judged blind against the source records for accuracy, completeness, citation quality, and safety, and defaults change only when results support it.

Study designs, results, and transcripts are published separately: WAHS evaluation reports ↗.

Limitations & Open Questions

What this approach does not yet address

  • Evaluation scope. Testing so far uses synthetic and de-identified records with blinded automated judges. Clinician-graded review and studies with real patients are still ahead.
  • Record quality. Condensation and retrieval depend on how sources code their data. Free-text-only results, inconsistent units, and missing dates limit what deterministic steps can recover.
  • Multi-step reasoning. Single-pass answering handles most lookup and synthesis questions, but questions that require reconciling long timelines across many documents remain difficult.
  • Client parity. The web app's upstream health record chat uses the engine but does not yet render interactive citations or apply per-resource selections.
  • Intended use. Answers help patients prepare for conversations with clinicians. They are not validated for clinical decision support.

WA Health Summary is an exploratory research and demonstration project using synthetic and de-identified records. AI outputs can contain errors and do not constitute medical advice or replace clinical review.
